# Inside the Health Beat: How Two Washington Reporters Track the Money, Cut the Jargon and Survive the News Cycle
By Brittany McAlister 
/ Graduate Studies Journalism Portfolio 
/ November 23, 2026

Wall Street Journal investigative reporter Liz Whyte. (Photo courtesy of Liz Whyte)

WASHINGTON, D.C. (GEORGETOWN UNIVERSITY) — Covering American health care from the nation’s capital means living in two worlds at once. On one side are dense federal regulations, Senate lobbying disclosures and multi-billion-dollar corporate mergers. On the other are patients, doctors and families dealing with the real-world fallout of those decisions.

To see how journalists navigate that divide, I spoke with two seasoned Washington health reporters: Alice Ollstein, a national health care reporter for [Politico](https://www.politico.com/staff/alice-miranda-ollstein), and Liz Whyte, an investigative health reporter for [The Wall Street Journal](https://www.wsj.com/news/author/liz-essley-whyte) who previously spent years covering long-term investigations at the Center for Public Integrity.

Across separate conversations, Ollstein and Whyte broke down how they translate policy for everyday readers, spot real news amid political theater and protect their own mental health on one of journalism’s most demanding beats.

The following interviews have been edited and condensed for length and clarity.

### Cutting Through Jargon and Corporate Spin

Whether dealing with legislative text or corporate executives, both reporters said clarity starts long before an article is published.

For Ollstein, writing for Politico’s broad readership requires stripping away Washington shorthand without losing precision.

"We try to avoid acronyms and use clear and straightforward terms and explanations without dumbing it down too much or editorializing, which can be a tough balance," Ollstein said. In a [recent](https://www.politico.com/news/2026/06/07/how-sick-is-sick-enough-new-medicaid-work-rule-worries-patient-advocates-states-00952476?_hsenc=p2ANqtz-9QmoAsq7JzZO0gmE8vifvoB-trwLBIu7jYNY19QjXKIkNoBQSoP4Fft8U8qWbYv8EO4ZoQddEavS2EnOvk-aVdl_qjRA&_hsmi=422745503) story on a new Medicaid work rule, for example, she and her editors chose the word "sick" instead of the government's official phrase, "medically frail," in several places.

When Whyte interviews pharmaceutical executives, lobbyists or hospital leaders, her biggest hurdle is getting past polished public relations talking points. Her solution is exhaustive homework before picking up the phone.

"If you do not really know which way is up when you are talking to someone — let us say it is a CEO and you do not have a sense of how their company has been doing the last few years — it is hard to press through talking points," Whyte said. "But if you are well-versed in the situation, how their company has been doing, or what they have been up to, then it is a little bit easier to say, 'Well, but this happened. What about that?'"

### Following the Money and Spotting the Story

In Washington, a flood of press releases, lawsuits and newly introduced bills hits reporters' inboxes every hour. Separating routine political theater from genuine news takes instinct and patience.

"Most of the time a bill being introduced in Congress or a lawsuit being filed is not, in my view, newsworthy," Ollstein said. "But there are exceptions to that if it is something very unusual, unexpected or influential. There also is not a binary to something being a story or not a story. Sometimes I will mentally file something away for later, or keep track of it to see if it becomes newsy down the road."

When Whyte wants to see who is quietly pushing a policy or drug behind the scenes, she heads straight to federal paper trails — starting with the Senate’s lobbying disclosure database, where lobbyists are legally required to report their clients and spending. Still, she noted that her best investigative tips rarely start with a spreadsheet.

"Most of my stories these days come from just talking to people and being on the phone a lot," Whyte said. "I call sources to ask what is going on in their corner of the world. You hear that a specific thing is happening, and then you follow up with other people to hear more about it."

A recent Journal story examining private equity firms buying up kidney care centers, she added, started simply from working the phones. As for knowing when a small tip points to a major scandal, Whyte relies on a rule of thumb from a colleague: "Look for stories that seep and ooze. That means it just seems like there is all kinds of fishy stuff going on, and not just a couple of things."

### Navigating Misinformation and Fairness

Health reporters today also have to contend with viral medical claims and polarized debates. Both journalists stressed that credibility depends on strict sourcing and proportionality.

Ollstein relies on a vetted network of researchers and nonpartisan institutions, making sure readers know who is behind every claim rather than asserting facts in her own voice. She also draws a hard line against using artificial intelligence for research, writing or fact-checking because of its high rate of errors and hallucinations.

When covering online health influencers or public figures spreading unverified medical claims, Whyte weighs reach and power before deciding whether to publish.

"Especially with public figures like Robert F. Kennedy Jr., some of his beliefs on vaccines are not founded or backed by science," Whyte said. "Part of the problem with him is that he already has the megaphone. He is a public figure, and he has a position of power, so we cannot ignore him entirely. I think that is a rule we have to use for other people, too. If it is a tiny account that is scaring five people, I am not going to give it a megaphone to talk to 50,000 more people."

On divisive policy fights, Ollstein said true fairness has nothing to do with counting quotes from opposing sides. "It is about being equally critical and rigorous no matter who you are talking to," she said. "If you think you have a completely black-and-white, solid opinion about an issue, it probably means you have not asked enough questions."

### Building Trust and Covering New Territory

Because health care intersects with immigration, the environment, technology and finance, reporters frequently find themselves stepping outside their comfort zones. Ollstein [leans](https://www.politico.com/news/2025/05/31/they-are-the-backbone-trumps-targeting-of-legal-immigrants-threatens-health-sector-00377579) on newsroom collaboration — partnering with colleagues on other beats — and never hesitates to call academics or think tank scholars to ask basic questions.

"Do not be embarrassed to do so," Ollstein said. "They usually are excited about talking to someone who is interested."

When approaching vulnerable sources such as patients or whistleblowers, Ollstein said radical transparency is nonnegotiable: explain what the story is about, clarify who else is being interviewed, ask permission before hitting record and offer anonymity only when someone faces a concrete risk, like losing their job.

### Survival Advice for New Reporters

Transitioning from long-term investigations at a nonprofit newsroom to daily deadlines at The Wall Street Journal taught Whyte to appreciate both rhythms — the depth of multi-month projects and the immediate satisfaction of filing daily news.

For students entering the field, Ollstein offered three practical rules for building a sustainable career:

* **Own your mistakes immediately:** "If you mess up, do not try to cover it up or minimize it. Bite the bullet and tell your editor, apologize and ask how to make it right. I have seen people get fired for things that could have been forgiven if they had just been honest about it from the start."
  
* **Protect your mental health:** "Make sure to have hobbies outside of work and friends who do not work in journalism. This is crucial for mental health. Also, take sick days and vacation days when you need them. Do not be a martyr and work yourself to death."
  
* **Support newsroom unions:** "Unions make a huge, positive difference and give journalists the stability to do their best work. If you get hired by an outlet with a union, join right away and get involved."

Ultimately, both reporters' experiences point to the same conclusion: whether tracking corporate money trails or translating federal rules for everyday readers, great health journalism comes down to thorough preparation, honest relationships and relentless phone reporting.
