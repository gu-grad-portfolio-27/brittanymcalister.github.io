Urban Farm Fights Chronic Illness East of Anacostia River by Trading Labor for Free Organic Food

By Brittany McAlister / July 15, 2026

A sign inside THEARC Farm reads, "THEARC Farm straight ahead!" The photo was taken in Southeast Washington, D.C., on July 14, 2026. (Brittany McAlister/Georgetown University)

WASHINGTON, D.C. (CONGRESS HEIGHTS), Jul. 14, 2026 — A critical shortage of volunteers at a Southeast Washington urban farm is threatening a vital public health pipeline that provides free organic produce to thousands of families in the city’s most under-resourced neighborhoods.

At the Town Hall Education Arts Recreation Campus, known as THEARC, agricultural staff and community members gathered Tuesday morning to pull weeds, prep soil, and harvest summer crops. The July 14 session, organized by the nonprofit Building Bridges Across the River, serves as a direct intervention against severe health disparities east of the Anacostia River. The farm operates a weekly free market to distribute its crops, but a steep drop in steady neighborhood labor has left the entire operation resting on the shoulders of just two full-time employees.

Public health data shows why the survival of the farm matters. According to D.C. Hunger Solutions, Ward 8 has only one full-service grocery store, a Giant on Alabama Avenue, serving an estimated 75,000 to 80,000 residents. This infrastructure gap creates a food desert where local families must rely on cheap, highly processed items from neighborhood corner stores. Doctors link these long-term dietary restrictions directly to the disproportionately high rates of diabetes, heart disease, stroke, and high blood pressure seen in Wards 7 and 8 compared to wealthier areas of the District.

The farm attempts to change these medical outcomes by supplying clean nutrition directly to the neighborhood. However, the manual labor required to maintain the 16.5-acre campus is stretching the staff to its physical limits.

J.J. Boone, the deputy director of THEARC Farm, grew up two streets away from the property and has spent the last 13 years cultivating its soil. He said the farm relies entirely on community participation to survive, though turnout fluctuates in unpredictable waves.

J.J. Boone, THEARC Farm’s deputy director, smiles for a photo in Southeast Washington, D.C., on July 14, 2026. (Brittany McAlister/Georgetown University)

"We rely heavily on volunteers to keep things going," Boone said. "I'm literally here every single day, Monday through Friday, and when the volunteers aren't here, it's just me. There have been times where we've been short or nobody shows up, and when that happens, the labor relies solely on me and my colleague. There is only so much two hands can get done."

Boone explained that federal and local safety net requirements sometimes drive temporary spikes in help. For instance, single adults receiving Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program, or SNAP benefits must complete a set number of community service hours to keep their food assistance. Boone recalled a 40-year-old neighborhood woman who spent over a week working the beds to fulfill her mandate before finding steady employment.

The food grown during these volunteer shifts goes toward the "Food and Farm Fridays" program, a free farmer's market operating from May through November. To ensure families can use raw items like zucchini, okra, and squash, the farm partners with organizations like the Capital Area Food Bank to offer recipe cards and live cooking demonstrations.

For the people working the dirt, the farm provides a dual benefit of immediate physical exercise and psychological relief from the surrounding concrete landscape.

Organic THEARC Farm potatoes are grown, pulled and prepared for distribution to feed residents of Wards 7 and 8 every Friday. The photo was taken in Southeast Washington, D.C., on July 14, 2026. (Brittany McAlister/Georgetown University)

Cameron Chohan, a local schoolteacher who moved to Anacostia with his wife two and a half years ago, spent his morning pulling potatoes and clipping okra. As an educator who teaches children from Wards 7 and 8, he sees how deep the nutritional divide runs between different parts of the city.

"Living in different places like Northeast near Benning Road, the Safeway there actually just closed," Chohan said. "It's funny to go there and then end up somewhere in Shaw by happenstance. It will be the same grocery chain, but have completely different quality produce. The big push has to be getting grocery stores with good produce across the entire city."

The Safeway at Hechinger Mall near Maryland Avenue and Benning Road NE closed May 16, ending more than 40 years of service to the community. Parent company Albertsons shut down the location upon the lease expiration, creating a significant food access gap in Northeast D.C.

Chohan noted that his school provides free and reduced-price lunches to help students get balanced meals, but expiration dates mean raw food often goes to waste. He routinely packages leftover apples and vegetables to send home to families who lack access to fresh food markets.

"It’s a good feeling knowing I just pulled a bunch of food that someone in this community is going to eat, and it’s not coming from somewhere else," Chohan said.

Other first-time volunteers joined the morning shift out of fear for the safety and longevity of the younger generation.

George Mitr, a Generation Z participant, arrived at the farm to study plant lifecycles and give back to his neighbors. He argued that the saturation of cheap and fatty choices in local storefronts combined with a total lack of public school nutrition classes has created an immediate medical crisis for young people.

"I saw one of my friends who ate junk food to the point that he had heart issues," Mitr said. "Once he started eating healthy, he got better, healthier, and more fit. Most Gen Z eat things like chicken nuggets, soda, and chips. It's because we're not taught health and nutrition in schools."
