# “They Shut Everything Down”: Aid Blocked at Gaza’s Border as Hunger Reaches Crisis Levels 

Humanitarian workers warn that sealed crossings, militia control, economic collapse and shrinking global attention are pushing millions toward the crisis. New United Nations data show Gaza now faces the most severe hunger in the Middle East.  

By Brittany McAlister 
/ August 12, 2026

WASHINGTON, D.C. (GEORGETOWN UNIVERSITY) — The hunger crisis in Gaza has reached a breaking point. Families are skipping meals, children are fainting in food lines, and aid trucks sit just miles away, unable to enter. For many humanitarian workers, the most painful part is knowing the food exists. But the people who need it most cannot reach it. 

“Israeli authorities control all the crossings so they shut them down and didn’t allow anyone to bring anything in,” said Shaina Low, a communications advisor for the [Norwegian Refugee Council (NRC)](https://www.nrc.no/75-years-helping-people-forced-to-flee). “We had 200 truckloads of aid sitting in Egypt. Some of it is still there.” 

Her words capture the urgency of a crisis that has quietly become the worst [hunger emergency in the Middle East](https://www.wfp.org/emergencies/middle-east-conflict). According to the Integrated Food Security Phase Classification (IPC), 30 percent of people in [North Gaza](https://www.ipcinfo.org/ipc-country-analysis/details-map/en/c/1156749/?iso3=PSE) were in Phase 5 — famine — between November 2023 and February 2024, and 45 percent were in Phase 4 — emergency. No other place in the region reported famine during that period. [Yemen’s Al Hodeidah](https://www.ipcinfo.org/ipc-country-analysis/details-map/en/c/1156803/?iso3=YEM) and [Lebanon’s Akkar](https://www.ipcinfo.org/ipc-country-analysis/details-map/en/c/1156728/?iso3=LBN) both reported zero famine, showing just how sharply Gaza stands apart. 

The World Food Program (WFP) warns that the Middle East is facing “[record levels of hunger](https://www.wfp.org/stories/why-middle-east-conflict-threatens-record-levels-hunger),” driven by conflict, economic collapse and blocked access to food. Gaza’s situation stands out: commercial supply lines have been shut down, farmland has been destroyed, and border access has dropped to some of its lowest levels in years. The United Nations Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs (OCHA) [reports](https://www.ochaopt.org/content/hostilities-gaza-strip-and-israel-flash-update-102) that aid convoys are routinely delayed or denied, leaving families with little more than bread, canned goods and whatever they can find in informal markets. 

In North Gaza, a staggering [67 percent of the population](https://www.ipcinfo.org/ipc-country-analysis/details-map/en/c/1165303/?iso3=PSE) is expected to face crisis or emergency hunger by 2026. Because tracking lines broke down during the initial chaos of late 2023, it is impossible to calculate the exact percentage increase, but the raw devastation is undeniable. Near Yemen’s Al Hodeidah, the emergency is also worsening, with extreme hunger projected to jump from 15 percent to 20 percent over the same period. This 5 percentage point increase represents thousands more families pushed to the brink of starvation. Aid groups warn that relentless conflict and economic collapse are simply moving faster than their ability to deliver lifesaving food. 

[These soaring survival](https://humanitarianaction.info/document/global-humanitarian-overview-2026) needs are quickly outrunning global funding, according to the United Nations (UN). The shortfall is driven in part by a shift in public empathy, with Pew Research reporting that most Americans believe U.S. foreign policy [ignores foreign interests](https://www.pewresearch.org/global/2026/04/28/most-americans-now-say-u-s-foreign-policy-ignores-the-interests-of-other-countries/). Humanitarian leaders say this political shift is already draining global aid budgets. As donor fatigue deepens, agencies warn that families trapped in Gaza, Yemen and Lebanon will ultimately pay the price through even deeper cuts to their daily bread.

This moment matters because the data shows a clear pattern: when aid spikes, hunger briefly improves; when access drops, hunger immediately worsens. And right now, access is at one of its lowest points since the conflict escalated. 

Low’s account offers rare, firsthand insight into how the shutdown has shaped the crisis. Her description of stalled trucks, blocked shipments, and shifting rules shows how the hunger emergency is tied directly to access restrictions. As Gaza’s food supplies run thin, the details help explain why humanitarian groups cannot reach families in need. 

Low said NRC once had more than 200 truckloads of aid waiting in Egypt, including education kits and other supplies meant for families displaced by the conflict. Some of those materials have been stuck for two years, unable to cross into Gaza. 

“It’s very expensive to keep trucks and truck drivers waiting,” Low said. Because the trucks could not remain at the border indefinitely, NRC moved the supplies into bonded warehouses, where goods are held under customs control. “They’re basically locked up to avoid tariffs and customs taxes,” she said on Signal. 

With no way to bring the shipments in directly, NRC donated portions of its aid to other organizations that were able to secure permission to enter Gaza. Once inside, those groups handed the supplies over to the NRC for distribution. In other cases, U.N. agencies brought in aid and transferred it to NRC teams already operating in the territory. 

“We’ve either had to donate them to other organizations or some of the aid is still waiting in warehouses in Egypt,” Low said.

Low told Georgetown University that the restrictions are not limited to border closures. Even organizations that are allowed to bring in aid face unpredictable rules about what items can enter. She said some supplies are authorized one week, blocked the next, and then reauthorized later, creating delays throughout the pipeline. 

The result, she said, is that most humanitarian groups in Gaza operate with only a few days’ worth of supplies at any given time. Stockpiling is nearly impossible. 

Movement inside Gaza is also incredibly dangerous. Low said roads are ruined, jammed, and terrifyingly unsafe for transport workers. She spoke heavily about a pattern of violence against coordinated aid runs, where drivers are targeted and killed while trying to deliver food. Among them was [Ahmad Nasser Salim](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/jul/09/idf-accused-of-field-execution-of-palestinian-driver-bringing-aid-into-gaza), a 30-year-old mechanic and driver shot by forces along the border after his convoy truck broke down and came to a sudden stop. 

The killing of Salim, also known as Ahmad Esleem, lays bare the constant threat of death hanging over the workers who try to get food to starving people. Salim was driving a food truck on a route fully cleared and pre-coordinated by the [UN World Food Program](https://www.wfp.org/emergencies/middle-east-conflict) when his engine failed. Witnesses said he got out to fix the breakdown and was standing with his hands up in compliance when he was shot at close range and left to die on the dirt road. His death caused deep anger and grief among logistics workers. Local driver associations called it a field execution and threatened to stop all aid deliveries because they have zero protection. 

“There’s a lot of insecurity for the truck drivers,” Low said. 

Humanitarian agencies say [Gaza’s border system](https://www.ochaopt.org/data/crossings) has become too fragile to support its population. The Erez crossing is fully closed, Rafah is either closed or damaged, and Kerem Shalom operates under heavy inspection. With only one partially functioning entry point, aid groups say the system cannot sustain the level of need.

Low said Israeli authorities now cite the NRC’s expired registration as the reason the group cannot bring in aid or international staff. But she explained that the registration process itself has been turned into a wall. 

“They basically have created a registration process that’s impossible for us to get through,” Low said, adding that they wanted to know all the local staff members and refused to tell us how the data would be used. 

Low went on to say that the NRC will remain barred from entering Gaza unless it completes the paperwork, but the group does not plan to resubmit the information and compromise its team. 

Instead, the restrictions seem to fall hardest on organizations that speak out about the suffering inside Gaza. Low noted that groups conducting public advocacy face significantly more roadblocks than others, signaling a deeper effort to control the narrative.

“It’s an attempt to stifle aid agencies that are reporting on what they’re seeing,” Low said. 

She added that this pattern is not new, but part of a long, painful campaign to choke off survival lines. 

“This is part of a strategy since the start of hostilities in 2023 to tighten and continue to create obstructions and restrictions preventing aid agencies from bringing in aid,” she said. 

While Gaza faces a near‑total shutdown of commercial supply lines, Yemen’s hunger crisis is shaped by a different set of barriers. In a separate interview, Hisham Al‑Omeisy — [Senior Yemen Advisor for the European Institute of Peace](https://www.eip.org/team-organisation/) — said Yemen’s conflict is driven by internal divisions, militia control and a decade‑long collapse of state institutions.

“You’re basically comparing apples to oranges here,” he said in a call on WhatsApp. “It’s a very different geopolitical situation in Gaza than it is in Yemen.” 

Al‑Omeisy told Georgetown that Yemen’s daily struggle for food is made even more heartbreaking by destroyed roads, remote villages, militia‑controlled cities, and dwindling funding. “We have aid agencies inside the country,” he said. “The problem is compounded by lack of funding, certain blockades, ongoing fighting, and the fact that some militias control some cities.”

He said Yemen’s conflict has lasted so long that global attention has faded. “Yemen has been dubbed the forgotten conflict,” he said. “With each year, there’s been less aid coming to Yemen.” 

Food‑aid deliveries across the Middle East dropped sharply between late 2023 and late 2024. In Gaza, monthly deliveries fell from 15,000 metric tons to about 1,000 within months of the border shutdown. Yemen saw deliveries fall from 85,000 metric tons to around 39,000, while Lebanon’s deliveries dipped from 12,000 to about 9,500 before recovering slightly later in 2024.

Aid groups say the declines reflect how fighting, border closures, and funding shortages have disrupted supply chains across the region. 

Al‑Omeisy said Yemen also sees sharp swings in food security when aid spikes or drops, similar to Gaza. “It is at the mercy of outside politics,” he said. He noted that some foreign powers refuse to deliver aid to areas controlled by certain factions. 

“It’s not just tied to aid,” he said. “It’s also tied to the logistics of delivering that aid.” 

Both Gaza and Yemen face economic collapse that makes food unaffordable even when it is available. In Gaza, UN reports show that local markets have been destroyed, leaving families unable to buy food even when small amounts reach shelves. 

Al‑Omeisy said [Yemen faces similar problems](https://www.wfp.org/emergencies/yemen-emergency), but the causes differ across its 21 governorates. “There is food, but people don't have the purchasing power,” he said. “Currency exchange rates skyrocketed. People see the cost for milk increasing, but only people at the top of the pyramid could purchase that milk.” 

He said unemployment has soared, businesses have shut down, and public workers have gone unpaid for years. “Teachers [and] doctors haven’t been paid for the past 10 years,” he said. 

Field reports from the [United Nations Children’s Fund (UNICEF) Australia](https://www.unicef.org.au/stories/from-deputy-principal-to-taxi-driver?srsltid=AfmBOoqcTR3ZCDEih7kfXEwE0_Ia6TQvKnYQ21nvWHK5_7UNyLuWdTH8) confirm Al-Omeisy’s claims, showing that roughly 171,600 educators — about two-thirds of the country’s teaching workforce — have not received a regular paycheck since 2016. This relentless wage freeze has forced thousands of broken-hearted professionals to walk away from their students to seek desperate day labor just to buy food for their own children. The collapse of income has left empty classrooms across the country, trapping millions of vulnerable children in total darkness without a future or an education. 

A [United Nations report](https://news.un.org/en/story/2026/06/1167643) confirms this grim reality, showing that nearly half the population in Yemen is trapped in a worsening state of severe hunger. UN teams warn that families are being pushed far past their breaking points by a ruined economy, missing paychecks and a steep drop in international funding. This loss of money has pulled vital food supplies away from parents at the exact moment they need them most to save their children. 

Both Gaza and Yemen have seen farmland and fishing infrastructure destroyed. In Gaza, greenhouses and fields have been wiped out, forcing reliance on imports. In Yemen, Al‑Omeisy said land mines planted by militias have contaminated soil and prevented farmers from reaching their land. 

“They’ve prevented farmers from going to their land,” he said. “They have also planted sea mines, which prevented fishermen from going out to sea.” 

He said the environmental impact has been “massive,” affecting groundwater, soil, and fisheries. 

Al‑Omeisy said global attention shifts from conflict to conflict, leaving a long‑running crisis behind. “It’s not about double standards,” he said. “It’s about urgency. Yemen has been in conflict for 11 years. Interest wanes with time.” 

He said Yemen was eclipsed by Syria, then Ukraine, and now Gaza. “It’s just the nature of the news cycle,” he said. 

When asked what he hopes to see in the next five years, Al‑Omeisy said one word: “Peace.” 

“Peace requires sacrifices,” he said. “It takes a brave man to seek peace. It takes a coward to seek war.” 

Low said she does not know when the NRC will be able to bring in aid again. “Most organizations in Gaza are operating with only a few days’ worth of supplies,” she said. 

Her final words showed just how large the crisis has become. 

“We had 200 truckloads of aid sitting in Egypt. Some of it is still there.”
