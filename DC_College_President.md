# When College Presidents Go Silent, One D.C. Leader Refuses to Step Back 
By Brittany McAlister 
/ Graduate Studies Journalism Portfolio
/ November 22, 2026 

<img width="2048" height="1169" alt="image-2" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/30a038ef-1878-4d6b-a8b7-23ea2a5a5311" />

Trinity Washington University president Patricia McGuire speaks during a Zoom call on March 27, 2026. (Screenshot by Brittany McAlister, Georgetown University journalism master's student)

WASHINGTON, D.C. (TRINITY WASHINGTON UNIVERSITY) — At a time when many college presidents are retreating from public debate, Patricia “Pat” McGuire is doing something increasingly rare in [higher education](https://www.usnews.com/news/national-news/articles/trumps-higher-education-crackdown-visa-revocations-dei-bans-lawsuits-and-funding-cuts): she’s raising her voice. 

On March 27, during a Zoom interview from her office at Trinity Washington University, McGuire adjusted her webcam, pushed a stack of papers just out of frame, and immediately launched into a critique of what she calls higher education’s “culture of caution.”

“Silence is not leadership,” she said. “Not now.” 

Across the country, college presidents are speaking less as [political pressure](https://www.chronicle.com/package/leadership-issue-2025), donor scrutiny, and new “institutional neutrality” policies reshape what leaders feel safe saying in public. By the end of 2024, at least 148 institutions serving roughly [2.6 million](https://content.heterodoxacademy.org/uploads/HxA_Statement-Neutrality-Report_FINAL.pdf) students had adopted neutrality rules, and national surveys show many presidents feel caught between lawmakers, donors, and campus constituencies. In a sector growing more cautious, McGuire stands out as one of the few presidents still willing to take public positions on issues of race, democracy, and social justice.

Meanwhile, an [Inside Higher Ed survey](https://www.aei.org/commentary/survey-reveals-college-and-university-presidents-failing-to-adopt-neutrality-policies/) reported that only 29% of presidents say their institution has a neutrality policy, while 66% say they do not and many of those leaders report feeling caught between political actors, donors, and campus constituencies. 

“Presidents are being told to stay in their lane,” said Provost Carlota Ocampo. “But the lane keeps getting narrower.”

Although neutrality policies are becoming more common, many students and even faculty don’t fully understand what they do. In practice, these policies restrict university leaders from making official statements on political, social, or global events unless the issue directly affects the institution’s operations.

Some universities, including the University of Chicago, Vanderbilt, and UNC Chapel Hill, have adopted versions of these rules. They argue that institutional silence protects academic freedom by preventing the university from taking sides.

But enforcement varies widely. At some campuses, presidents are discouraged from commenting on anything beyond campus safety or direct institutional impact. At others, the policies are more symbolic than binding.

Experts say the trend accelerated after the 2024 election and the rise of federal scrutiny on higher education. According to an [analysis](https://msutoday.msu.edu/news/2025/02/trump-white-house-actions-to-date-msu-experts-can-comment) published by Michigan State University’s Education Policy Innovation Collaborative, which tracks federal actions affecting universities, say the Trump administration’s early actions — including investigations into DEI programs, threats to research funding, and pressure on universities over campus protests — have pushed many institutions into “risk‐avoidance mode.” MSU experts note that universities are now navigating “a political environment where any public statement can trigger consequences.”

That environment has made neutrality feel safer for many presidents. But critics argue it also leaves students without moral or institutional guidance during moments of crisis.

For many students, neutrality doesn’t feel neutral at all.

“A lot of the silence we see right now is disheartening,” said Ashwath Tirunellayi, a rising senior in Georgetown University’s School of Health. “Students want to see pushback when funding or research is threatened. When leaders say nothing, it feels like we’re on our own.”

Tirunellayi said neutrality can make sense in small matters, but not when core values or student well‐being are at stake.

“Neutrality as a default isn’t always bad,” he said. “But if you overdo it, you create more tension. There are moments when neutrality goes against the values the university claims to uphold.”

He pointed to a recent Georgetown controversy as an example.

“The divestment referendum passed overwhelmingly, and within twenty minutes the administration sent an email saying they wouldn’t even consider it,” he said. “You could feel the tension on campus. Students felt ignored.”

Students also pay close attention to the statements universities do release.

“When a statement conflicts with what people believe is right, you see protests, club statements, social media — the whole campus reacts,” Tirunellayi said. “Students are watching.”

But McGuire, a Trinity alum, Georgetown Law graduate, and one of the longest‐serving presidents in the region, has spent decades doing the opposite. Her outspokenness has made her an outlier in a profession that increasingly rewards caution. 

In recent years, college presidents have been criticized from all sides: for speaking too much, for speaking too little, for speaking too late. The result, according to national surveys, is a growing reluctance among leaders to comment on issues ranging from immigration to racial justice to threats against democracy.

Ocampo has watched the shift firsthand. “There’s fear; fear of backlash, fear of political consequences, fear of losing funding,” she said. “It’s easier to say nothing.”

Tirunellayi sees those pressures too.

“Presidents are balancing donors, lawmakers, and students and those pressures often conflict,” he said. “But I want a university that represents its students publicly, not just behind closed doors.”

He said silence can become a cycle.

“If you stay silent when research and education are being cut, it becomes easier for the next cut to happen,” he said.

Some presidents argue that speaking out can alienate parts of the community or expose the institution to political retaliation. Tirunellayi acknowledges that risk, especially at a place like Georgetown.

“A university like Georgetown is politically connected, so the backlash is real,” he said. “But living up to your mission means speaking even when it’s hard.”

But McGuire has never been comfortable with “easy.”

McGuire has led Trinity since 1989, transforming it from a struggling Catholic women’s college into a predominantly [Black and Latina institution](https://discover.trinitydc.edu/about/trinity-dare-driving-actions-for-racial-equity/) centered on access and equity. Her leadership has always been intertwined with activism on racial justice, immigration, women’s rights, and academic freedom.

“She is not ducking and running,” Ocampo said. “She stands firm in her truth.”

That stance has come with consequences. After McGuire publicly criticized federal immigration policies in 2018, she received a wave of angry emails. Some from alumni who accused her of politicizing the university.

McGuire shrugged when recalling it. “If you’re going to speak up, you have to accept that not everyone will clap for you,” she said, lifting a chipped purple Trinity mug briefly into the Zoom frame.

But she believes the greater risk is staying quiet.

“Higher education has a responsibility to defend democracy,” she said. “If presidents won’t say that, who will?”

For many students, the silence from university leaders feels personal.

Amy Lin‐Truong, a graduate student in Georgetown University’s McCourt School of Public Policy, said presidents have an obligation to speak clearly during moments of political tension.

“It’s really important for university presidents to articulate clear statements about how they plan to support their student body, especially during times of heightened political unrest,” she said. “The climate is already hostile for many students. Leaders need to make sure students feel like they belong on their campuses.”

She said that commitment is especially critical for students of color.

“Students need to feel that their presence and their dignity are affirmed,” she said.

Lin‐Truong noted that the current political environment has made some leaders hesitant to speak out.

“With the current administration targeting universities that pursue more progressive initiatives — especially DEI programs — I get it, things can be difficult,” she said. “But that doesn’t mean you back down from your values. For lack of a better phrase, you have to grow a spine.”

Karam, a Palestinian advocate at the Palestine House of Freedom who asked that his full name not be used due to his undocumented status, said financial pressures also shape presidential silence.

“Tuition keeps going up. It never goes down, and universities are generating so much money from it,” he said. “Presidents worry that if they speak out on certain issues, their funding will be cut.”

But he argued that silence undermines the very mission of higher education.

“For anything to change or even to be looked at seriously, they have to stand up,” he said. “By staying silent, nothing changes. And the funding they’re trying to protect becomes meaningless if most of their students disagree with the very thing they’re afraid to address.”

Tirunellayi agreed, saying leadership shapes campus climate.

“When leaders speak on hot‐button issues, it changes how students feel heard or not heard by the university,” he said.

McGuire’s activism is not just rhetorical. It shows up in policy, partnerships, and the daily culture of the university.

During the COVID‐19 pandemic, she directed federal emergency funds to students rather than the institution — a decision that still stands out to Martha Molina, Trinity’s director of financial aid.

“We could have used it for the school,” Molina said. “But she opened those funds to the students. Many used it to pay off balances. That’s who we are.”

Financial aid director Martha Molina sits at her desk in her office at Trinity Washington University in Washington on Sept. 23, 2024. (Photo by Brittany McAlister, Georgetown University journalism master's student)

Her advocacy has also shaped Trinity’s academic direction. Dean Sita Ramamurti said McGuire’s leadership pushed the College of Arts & Sciences to dismantle traditional academic barriers and expand access for first‐generation and underrepresented students.

“Academic excellence and accessibility are not mutually exclusive,” Ramamurti said. “Under her guidance, we’ve dismantled preparation gaps, cultural barriers, inflexible pathways and systemic inequities.”

These changes reflect a broader national conversation: Who is higher education for? And who gets to define its mission?

The pressure on presidents to remain neutral has intensified as political leaders target universities over diversity programs, curriculum content, and campus speech. Some institutions have responded by adopting “institutional neutrality” policies that discourage leaders from speaking out at all.

But neutrality, McGuire argues, is not neutral.

“When you stay silent about injustice, you’re taking a position,” she said.

Students and faculty feel the impact of that silence. Ocampo said McGuire’s willingness to speak publicly gives others permission to do the same.

“She models intellectual courage,” Ocampo said. “That matters for faculty. It matters for students. It matters for the city.”

Tirunellayi echoed that sentiment.

“To be neutral, a president has to call things as they are,” he said. “You can’t pretend both sides are equal when the facts aren’t.”

As higher education faces [political attacks](https://www.aaup.org/issues-higher-education/political-attacks-higher-education), enrollment challenges, and debates over its purpose, McGuire’s leadership offers a counter‐model to the trend of presidential quietism.

“Future deans and academic leaders can learn from her example,” Ramamurti said. “Align the mission with societal needs. Prioritize access and inclusion without compromising academic rigor.”

Molina put it more simply: “She taught us how to stand up.”

McGuire has not announced plans to step down, though she acknowledges the question is coming.

“There will come a time where I will ride off into the sunset,” she said. “But I’m not ready to make any announcements quite yet.”

Whenever that time comes, her colleagues say her influence will continue not just at Trinity, but in the broader conversation about what higher‐education leadership should look like.

In an era when many college presidents are choosing silence, McGuire continues to speak loudly, clearly, and unapologetically.

“She inspires others to stand ten toes down for what we know is right,” Ocampo said.

And in a sector struggling to find its voice, that may be the lesson that endures.
