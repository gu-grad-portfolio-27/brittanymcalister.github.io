# ‘We’re Paying the Price for Their Policies’: Ward 5 Residents Struggle With High Costs for Household Essentials
By Brittany McAlister 
/ November 22, 2026 

<img width="1600" height="1200" alt="Ward_5_Image1" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/7d0a296e-49c8-4d12-89c4-fb8ea76965cd" />

WASHINGTON, D.C. (NOMA) — In Washington’s Ward 5, rising costs on everyday household goods are straining family budgets. Residents say corporate pricing decisions and federal tariffs have driven up the cost of essentials like toilet paper, laundry detergent and dish soap, forcing difficult choices at the checkout counter.

“I’m not asking for luxury,” said George Porter, 61. “I’m just trying to stay clean. But when detergent costs more than a meal, something’s wrong.”

Ward 5 resident George Porter sits on a bench in Washington's Eckington neighborhood on a warm, sunny afternoon on Aug. 8, 2025. (Photo by Brittany McAlister, Georgetown University journalism master's student)

Porter, a retired maintenance worker who has lived in Ward 5 for more than 30 years, said store prices have climbed faster and more unpredictably than he has ever seen.

“I used to get a bottle of Tide for $10. Now it’s $13 or $14, depending on the store,” Porter said. “And that’s before tax.”

The price increases stem from broader economic pressures, including federal tariffs that raise the cost of raw materials and manufacturing. Consumer goods giant [Procter & Gamble](https://supplychainbrain.com) has cited tariff-related expenses as a primary driver behind price hikes across its product lines. For Ward 5 residents, corporate explanations offer little relief.

Marquisha Williams, 29, said inflation, stagnant wages and higher retail prices have left her feeling trapped.

“My cost of living has gone up by nearly 80%,” Marquisha Williams said. “But my income hasn’t changed. I’m constantly choosing between hygiene and food.”

Marquisha Williams, who holds a master’s degree in public policy, experienced a prolonged stretch of unemployment and relied on local shelters and food pantries to get by. She said even those safety nets are stretched thin.

“Donations are down. Shelters are full. And now basic items like toilet paper are becoming luxury goods,” she said. “We’re paying the price for their policies.”

Single mother Queen Barnes, 33, said she has cut back on basic household supplies to make ends meet.

“I used to buy paper towels and cleaning products every week,” Barnes said. “Now I wait until something runs out completely. Even then, I have to think twice.”

Barnes said she believes federal tariffs have given major companies cover to raise prices without pushback.

“They say it’s about supply chains and tariffs, but it feels like they’re just taking advantage,” she said. “And we’re the ones suffering.”

For Anjali Garg, 48, a federal employee with nearly 20 years of government service, agency disruptions and administrative leave have compounded the stress of a higher cost of living. Even while continuing to receive a paycheck, she said the instability weighs on her mental health and her neighborhood.

“The shutdowns and legal battles are hurting everyone,” Garg said. “And when prices go up, it’s not just about money — it’s about dignity.”

Garg said the strain has left her questioning her future in public service. “I used to believe in the system,” she said. “Now I’m not so sure.”

David Williams, 48, said the struggle to afford basic items cuts to a deeper issue.

“This is about respect,” David Williams said. “When you can’t afford toilet paper, it’s not just inconvenient — it’s humiliating.”

David Williams said policymakers and corporate leaders need to consider how their decisions play out in local neighborhoods.

“They need to stop thinking like executives and start thinking like neighbors,” he said. “We’re doing everything we can, but we need help.”

Procter & Gamble has defended its pricing decisions, citing [higher costs](https://supplychainbrain.com) for transportation, packaging and raw materials tied to tariffs. In Ward 5, residents say those justifications do not reflect what families face daily.

“We’re not asking for handouts,” Porter said. “We’re asking for fairness.”

Community advocates warn that high costs for basic goods feed into wider problems across the ward, including [food insecurity, housing instability and mental health strain](https://frac.org). Residents say they want targeted relief, including expanded Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program benefits and stronger oversight of how essential goods are priced.

“We need policies that protect people, not just profits,” David Williams said.

As household budgets remain tight, Ward 5 residents continue to speak out about what it takes to cover the basics.

“We’re paying the price for decisions we didn’t make,” Barnes said. “And we deserve better.”
