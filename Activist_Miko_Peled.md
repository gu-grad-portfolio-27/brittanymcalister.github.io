# From General’s Son to Capitol Hill Activist: Miko Peled’s Unwavering Fight for Palestinian Freedom
By Brittany McAlister 
/ May 10, 2026

<img width="2048" height="1536" alt="Miko_Peled_Image1" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/1b8d3081-e74d-4dea-a38d-10fc8087c6f7" />

Miko Peled signs a copy of his book in his office at the Palestine House of Freedom during a Mother’s Day and Eid celebration in Washington on March 21, 2026. (Photo by Brittany McAlister/Georgetown University)

WASHINGTON — When Miko Peled logged onto Zoom from his office on Pennsylvania Avenue SE on March 16, he spoke with the calm certainty of someone who has spent decades challenging the story he was raised to believe. The 64-year-old Israeli-American activist, once the son of a revered [Israeli general](https://amazon.com) and now one of the most outspoken critics of Israeli policy, says his life’s work is rooted in a simple realization: “What changed was what I knew. I didn’t change inherently. I realized I didn’t have possession of the truth at all.”

But in Washington’s Capitol Hill neighborhood, Peled’s work is no longer defined only by his personal transformation. It is anchored in a physical space — the [Palestine House of Freedom](https://daralhurriya.org), a community center he opened earlier this year on Pennsylvania Avenue. The center has quickly become a hub for lectures, film screenings, cultural events and political organizing.

It has also become a gathering place for Palestinian families in the region. Muna, a Palestinian mother who attended the March 21 [Mother’s Day and Eid celebration](https://daralhurriya.org/events/mothers-day-eid-celebration) with her three-year-old son, said the space fills a cultural and emotional need for families like hers. She spoke on the condition that only her first name be used because she fears harassment under the Trump administration and works at the Kennedy Center.

Palestinian and District of Columbia flags hang side by side on the exterior of the Palestine House of Freedom in Washington on March 21, 2026. (Photo by Brittany McAlister/Georgetown University)

“Being part of the Palestinian diaspora, it is critically important for us to be able to pass our culture on to the next generation,” she said. “We brought my son today so that he can celebrate his Palestinian culture and the beauty of it.”

For Muna, the center offers something she rarely finds elsewhere in Washington — a place where her son can grow up connected to his heritage.

“I want him to take pride in his Palestinian identity,” she said. “To know that he is part of shaping the future of Palestine, and that the struggle for freedom will always continue through Palestinian generations.”

She learned about the event from a friend who had a booth at the celebration, but said the decision to attend was immediate.

“It sounded like a great reason to come out,” she said.

To longtime Washington figures, the center represents more than another activist space. [Thomas Getman](https://sojo.net), a former Capitol Hill and White House staffer who has worked in international affairs for decades, said Peled’s arrival has shifted the landscape.

“Miko’s work here in Washington is based on a very good reputation that brought him here to do a very specialized intervention,” Getman said. “By opening the House for Palestine on our main street here on Capitol Hill … it’s a precursor to a return of a proper embassy for the Palestinian people.”

Getman said the center’s presence has been surprisingly smooth for such a politically charged topic.

“Because he has such a good reputation, far and wide … it has been a blessing to us to not have a lot of controversy around this very important new business,” he said. “The programming has been excellent — films, lectures, parties, meetings. It’s a welcome addition to our little community.”

Peled grew up in a household steeped in Israeli military and political life. His father, Matti Peled, was a general in the 1967 war; his grandfather signed Israel’s Declaration of Independence. But the stories he heard — and the ones he didn’t — shaped a worldview he would later dismantle.

His political shift began in the early 2000s, when he joined dialogue groups in California and heard Palestinians describe their families’ displacement. “They couldn’t all be lying,” he said. “And what they were saying was the exact opposite of everything I thought I knew.”

One of the most defining moments in Peled’s activism came at a West Bank checkpoint, where soldiers pulled him off a bus and held him for hours in a freezing, open-air room.

“I’m white, I’m a guy, I’m Israeli — I’m as privileged as you can be,” he said. “If that’s what they feel comfortable doing to me, I can’t imagine what it’s like for a Palestinian kid.”

Peled’s discipline — shaped by decades of practicing and teaching karate — has guided him through arrests, confrontations and protests. “My instinct is to respond physically, but something stops me,” he said. “The wise thing is to have discipline.”

Getman, who has spoken alongside Peled at national conferences, said that discipline extends beyond physical restraint.

“He creates an atmosphere of trust,” Getman said. “He not only calls attention to his concerns, but by the way he facilitates his co-panelists — even people who oppose his point of view — he makes space for them. Just his presence in a room has power to it.”

Over the past two years, Peled has visited student encampments across the country, including at [George Washington University](https://gwhatchet.com), Columbia and UCLA. He described the student-led protests as unlike anything he has seen in decades of activism.

“What I saw is the most incredible display of humanity I have ever seen,” he said.

But Peled’s presence on campuses has not always been welcomed. At [Georgetown University](https://thehoya.com) several years ago, some students protested his appearance, calling his views polarizing.

Students who opposed his appearance cited concerns about his criticism of Zionism and past statements they viewed as inflammatory.

Getman said he has seen Peled respond to criticism with persistence rather than retreat.

“He hangs in there,” Getman said. “If there’s a controversy, he circles back in. You row round and round the island until you find a place to land — that’s what he does here in Washington.”

Peled’s activism is shaped by lessons from Palestinian organizers he has worked with for decades. One friend, a grassroots leader who has been jailed repeatedly, once told him: “We expect to pay a price. When we resist, we expect to pay a price. So when it happens, we’re ready for it.”

Peled paused before adding his own conclusion: “If you want to stand up for something, you need to expect to pay a price and if you’re not willing to, get out of the way.”

Getman believes this moment — politically and culturally — is uniquely suited to Peled’s message.

“There’s never been a moment like today,” he said. “People are more aware of what’s happening in Gaza and across the region. It’s a perfect time for people like Miko to have his message heard.”

Whether speaking to students, writing or organizing in Washington, Peled sees his activism as a continuation — not a rejection — of the values he was raised with.

“The values were there,” he said. “I just realized I was rooting for the wrong side.”

For Peled, the work is far from over. But he believes the current moment is pivotal.

“This is a unique time,” he said. “People need to use their privilege, find their niche and fight.”
